The Human C-Suite: AI agent skills for running a company without losing your humanity

Open-source agent skills that make an AI agent think like a humane CEO, CFO, CTO, CPTO and CHRO: grounded in company data, layoffs as the last resort, AI measured before anyone banks on it, and pushback welcome.

Jimmy Nguyen · 20 November 2026

We don't have an AI problem. We have a leadership problem.

AI just makes the bad decisions faster.

Through October 2026, US employers blamed AI for 121,401 announced job cuts, more than double the total for all of 2025 (HCAmag on Challenger, Gray & Christmas). Most bad calls aren't made by bad people. They're made under pressure: in one survey, 78% of financial executives said they would give up long-term value to report smoother earnings (Graham, Harvey & Rajgopal).

Good leadership isn't soft. It's a strategy that works.

  • Costco paid well above retail norms and told Wall Street it was building a company for the next 50 to 60 years (RetailWire on The New York Times).
  • Lincoln Electric has guaranteed work to its long-serving people since 1958. Since it went public in 1995, its stock is up 3,834% against 740% for the S&P 500 (Fortune).
  • Nintendo's president Satoru Iwata halved his own pay when the 3DS and later the Wii U disappointed (Game Informer), and refused to lay people off: people who fear for their jobs, he said, won't make games that impress the world (Siliconera). The teams stayed together, and the Switch became Nintendo's best-selling console (Nintendo).
  • Market Basket's employees and customers shut the chain down for six weeks until the CEO who shared the profits was back in charge (WBUR).

The opposite costs everyone. In the six years leading up to the 737 MAX crisis, Boeing spent $43.4 billion on share buybacks and $15.7 billion on research and development for commercial airplanes (Fortune).

So I built the leaders we wish we had, as AI agents. The Human C-Suite is a set of free, open-source agent skills: a CEO, a CFO, a CTO and a CPTO that treat layoffs as the last rung, have leaders take the first cut and own the result, go to the real data before they advise, bring people in while the plan can still change, and end every answer with "what am I missing?" They fit any kind of organisation: a startup on a tight runway, a family business, a listed or fund-owned company, a non-profit or a public body, and frontline work in factories, shops, care and warehouses as well as offices. In blind tests on Gemini 4 Neon, they took the agent from 51 to 80 of 84 checks.

This page is the short version, told in four short videos. The full test results and every source are in the README on GitHub.

Why it matters

A layoff looks cheap because only half of its cost is on the slide. Severance comes first; rehiring, lost know-how and worse service come later. A 1% downsizing was linked to 31% more voluntary turnover the next year, so you also lose people you meant to keep (Trevor & Nyberg, 2008). For the people let go it isn't only income: in one US study, mortality in the year after a mass layoff was 50 to 100% higher than expected (Sullivan & von Wachter, 2009).

Treating people well doesn't cost shareholders either. Fortune's "Best Companies to Work For" beat the market by 3.5% a year from 1984 to 2009 (Edmans, 2011), and the most engaged business units were 23% more profitable (Gallup, 2024). These are correlations, not proof, but they point the same way. And the alternatives exist:

  • IKEA let an AI assistant take routine calls, then retrained about 8,500 call-centre workers as remote interior design advisers. That business brought in about €1.3 billion in 2022 (Forrester).
  • Barry-Wehmiller faced layoffs in 2009 and chose shared sacrifice instead: four weeks of unpaid furlough for everyone and no executive bonuses. Fiscal 2010 became a record year (Barry-Wehmiller).

One caveat on Nintendo's story: a pay cut at the top can't save a broken business on its own. What it does is show who carries the cost first, and that buys the trust a hard plan needs.

How the seats think

There are five seats. the-human-ceo handles cost cuts, reorganisations, AI rollouts and board papers. the-human-cfo handles budgets, savings and layoff business cases, cash and the bank, buybacks and bonus plans. the-human-cto handles AI coding tools, platforms, tech debt and engineering metrics. the-human-cpto handles roadmaps, features sales has promised, and the AI story for the board. the-human-chro handles reviews and ranking, who is selected when cuts are unavoidable, return to office, pay gaps, AI in hiring and burnout. Each works on its own, and when a question crosses seats they bring in each other's view. They share a few habits:

  • Layoffs are the last rung. Every cost problem goes down a ladder from least to most human harm: waste and spend, revenue and pricing, stopping low-value work, removing toil, a hiring freeze, redeploying and reskilling, leaders taking the first cut, voluntary exits, and only then involuntary cuts.
  • The whole cost, in the right quarter. A cut that saves €9 million on paper can cost money in its first year: exit costs come first, savings later. So every saving is shown net of its costs and quarter by quarter, the levers that cost no jobs are sized first, a "one-off" charge has to happen once, and a bonus plan puts leaders' pay at risk before anyone else's job.
  • Real data, not guesses. With your tools connected through Model Context Protocol (MCP) (Jira, Confluence, GitHub, the data warehouse, cloud billing, dashboards), they look for savings that don't cost jobs. People data stays aggregated, and nobody is ranked on story points, pull requests or AI usage.
  • Fits the organisation in front of it. A startup's runway, a family firm's next generation, a fund's exit, a non-profit's mission and a municipality's public trust: each gets the long-term case in its own terms. Rosters, quotas and staffing levels in shops, warehouses and care count as people decisions too.
  • Reasons, not rank. A title doesn't make an argument right, the agent's own included. Every answer shows its reasoning and ends with "what am I missing?". A challenge counts as free risk analysis, and people accept hard decisions more readily when they were heard (Kim & Mauborgne).
  • No surprises. The people affected see the problem and the options while they can still shape them.
  • Borrowed from Europe and the Nordics. For a dip that will pass: reduced hours or short-time work before cuts. The works council comes in while its advice can still change the plan. Support starts on the day of notice, as Sweden's job security councils do; over 85% of displaced Swedish workers had a new job within a year (OECD).
  • Leaders go first. When results disappoint, a leader says plainly that the result is theirs and takes the first and largest cut, before any frontline job: a signal of trust, not the savings plan. No one is offered up as the answer to a system failure.
  • Short answers for people who have to act. An engineer or product manager facing a decision from above gets the first steps, starting with a private conversation, and a line they can use, in about 400 words rather than a briefing.
  • Coaching, not lecturing. Many requests are shortcuts: a big number by a near date, or "just give me a yes or no". The seats answer what can safely be done now, name the trap in one sentence and ask the one question that would change the answer.

The habits work without AI too. A leadership toolkit has four templates to copy: a decision record, a "how we decide here" page, a no-surprises agreement and a short pulse survey.

Does it work?

I tested the skills with Anthropic's skill-creator: the same requests with and without the skill, on Claude and on Gemini, each answer checked against a list of what a good answer contains. The grading is blind: the grader doesn't know which answers used the skill.

The honest headline first: plain Claude already has most of these values. On simple requests it passed 22 of 23 checks without the skill. What the skill changes is how the agent acts once the facts are on the table:

The CFO seat had to earn its place. On six finance cases (an 8% cut for every cost centre, a model for 120 redundancies, a bonus paid on adjusted EBITDA, a buyback to rescue earnings guidance, a covenant breach and an AI business case for the back office), it had to beat the CEO seat on every model I tested. It did: 126 of 144 checks against 113 on Claude and Gemini together, and a higher overall-quality score on all four models. One run per setup, so the lead is modest but consistent (the CFO test).

The CHRO seat had to earn its place the same way. On six people cases (forced ranking with a 10% quota, AI screening that rejects 70% of applicants unseen, five days back in the office, a pay gap the CFO wants kept quiet, a burnout survey the CEO wants called stable, and a layoff list ranked on sick days and part-time work), it had to beat the CEO seat on Claude Sonnet and Claude Opus. It did: 34 of 36 checks against 27 on Sonnet, and 36 of 36 against 33 on Opus, where the lead is small. Where it fell short, it was fixed and tested again. Asked to announce five days back in the office, it now writes a proposal for the works council instead of a decision, and offers what takes nothing away from people first: funded team days, a home-working allowance and a proper desk, chair and screen (the CHRO test).

It isn't only for Claude. On Gemini, through Google's Antigravity, the skill made an even bigger difference. With it, Gemini made no factual errors in 28 runs, against 6 without, and had a leader own a bad result in 14 of 16 runs where something had gone wrong, against 4 of 16 (all runs and grades).

It wasn't better everywhere. Plain models already handle some cases well, and there the skill barely moves the score. The tests also found weak spots in the skill itself, such as a reference file that made Gemini send any disagreement to the confidential adviser; those are fixed and retested. Each comparison used two or three runs per setup, so read the numbers as direction, not precision. And the skill costs tokens: 9 to 22% more on Claude, roughly double on Gemini.

So I'd call it a stance and a checklist, not a smarter analyst. It doesn't make the model find more; it makes the agent act more consistently like the leader you'd want. The test cases ship in the repo, so you can rerun them on your own model.

Install and use it

The skills live at github.com/Stukongeluk/the-human-c-suite. In Claude Code, install all four seats as a plugin:

/plugin marketplace add Stukongeluk/the-human-c-suite
/plugin install the-human-c-suite@the-human-c-suite

Or copy a folder from skills/ into ~/.claude/skills/. In the Claude apps, zip a seat's folder and upload it under Customize > Skills (current steps). Then just ask: "The board wants 15% savings, what are our options?" The skills follow the open Agent Skills format, so other agents can load them too; I've tested them on Claude and on Gemini through Google's Antigravity.

What it is not

It's not legal, financial or HR advice: it flags where employment law and works councils come in, and people decide. It's not a way to make layoffs look nicer, and not a surveillance tool. And it doesn't replace leadership: an agent can gather the data and ask the uncomfortable questions, but looking people in the eye is still a human job.

I'm an engineer, not a CEO. I believe the tools we build should make companies better places to work. So try it on your next budget round or reorganisation and tell me where it falls short. Issues and pull requests are welcome; in the spirit of the skills, pushback is the point. And if you think leaders should work like this, star the repo and send it to one person who signs off on budgets.
