The Human C-Suite: AI agent skills for running a company without losing your humanity

Open-source agent skills that make an AI agent think like a humane CEO, CTO and CPTO: grounded in company data, layoffs as the last resort, AI measured before anyone banks on it, and pushback welcome.

Jimmy Nguyen · 20 November 2026

Most cost-cutting decks end on the same slide: a headcount number. It is the fastest lever to pull and the easiest to explain to shareholders. It is also the one that costs people their income, and sometimes their health.

AI is now the favourite reason. Through October 2026, US employers cited AI in 121,401 announced job cuts, more than double the total for all of 2025 (HCAmag on Challenger, Gray & Christmas). Yet the labour data doesn't show AI replacing workers en masse. Often "AI" is the story told about a budget decision.

So I built The Human C-Suite: free, open-source agent skills that make an AI agent think like a humane CEO, CTO and CPTO. They start from the company's data, treat layoffs as the last resort, measure AI before anyone banks on it, and want to be told when they're wrong.

This page is the short version, told in four short videos. The full test results and every source are in the README on GitHub.

Why it matters

A layoff looks cheap because only half of its cost is on the slide. Severance comes first; rehiring, lost know-how and worse service come later. A 1% downsizing was linked to 31% more voluntary turnover the next year, so you also lose people you meant to keep (Trevor & Nyberg, 2008). For the people let go it isn't only income: in one US study, mortality in the year after a mass layoff was 50 to 100% higher than expected (Sullivan & von Wachter, 2009).

Treating people well doesn't cost shareholders either. Fortune's "Best Companies to Work For" beat the market by 3.5% a year from 1984 to 2009 (Edmans, 2011), and the most engaged business units were 23% more profitable (Gallup, 2024). These are correlations, not proof, but they point the same way. And the alternatives exist:

  • IKEA let an AI assistant take routine calls, then retrained about 8,500 call-centre workers as remote interior design advisers. That business brought in about €1.3 billion in 2022 (Forrester).
  • Barry-Wehmiller faced layoffs in 2009 and chose shared sacrifice instead: four weeks of unpaid furlough for everyone and no executive bonuses. Fiscal 2010 became a record year (Barry-Wehmiller).
  • Nintendo's president Satoru Iwata halved his own pay when the 3DS and later the Wii U disappointed, and kept the teams together (Game Informer). People who fear for their jobs, he said, won't make games that impress the world (Siliconera). The Switch became Nintendo's best-selling console (Nintendo).

A pay cut at the top can't save a broken business on its own. What it does is show who carries the cost first, and that buys the trust a hard plan needs.

How the seats think

There are three seats. the-human-ceo handles cost cuts, reorganisations, AI rollouts and board papers. the-human-cto handles AI coding tools, platforms, tech debt and engineering metrics. the-human-cpto handles roadmaps, features sales has promised, and the AI story for the board. Each works on its own, and when a question crosses seats they bring in each other's view. They share a few habits:

  • Layoffs are the last rung. Every cost problem goes down a ladder from least to most human harm: waste and spend, revenue and pricing, stopping low-value work, removing toil, a hiring freeze, redeploying and reskilling, leaders taking the first cut, voluntary exits, and only then involuntary cuts.
  • Real data, not guesses. With your tools connected through MCP (Jira, Confluence, GitHub, the data warehouse, cloud billing, dashboards), they look for savings that don't cost jobs. People data stays aggregated, and nobody is ranked on story points, pull requests or AI usage.
  • Reasons, not rank. A title doesn't make an argument right, the agent's own included. Every answer shows its reasoning and ends with "what am I missing?". A challenge counts as free risk analysis, and people accept hard decisions more readily when they were heard (Kim & Mauborgne).
  • No surprises. The people affected see the problem and the options while they can still shape them.
  • Borrowed from Europe and the Nordics. For a dip that will pass: reduced hours or short-time work before cuts. The works council comes in while its advice can still change the plan. Support starts on the day of notice, as Sweden's job security councils do; over 85% of displaced Swedish workers had a new job within a year (OECD).
  • Coaching, not lecturing. Many requests are shortcuts: a big number by a near date, or "just give me a yes or no". The seats answer what can safely be done now, name the trap in one sentence and ask the one question that would change the answer.

New in version 1.3

  • Leaders go first and own the result. When results disappoint, a leader says plainly that it's theirs and takes the first and largest cut, before any frontline job: a signal of trust, not the savings plan. No one is offered up as the answer to a system failure.
  • Short answers for people who have to act. An engineer or product manager facing a decision from above gets the first steps and a line they can use, in about 400 words, not a briefing.
  • Usable without AI. A leadership toolkit with four templates to copy: a decision record, a "how we decide here" page, a no-surprises agreement and a short pulse survey.
  • Sharper on European rules for the Netherlands, Germany and Sweden, plus seven new test cases.

Does it work?

I tested every version with Anthropic's skill-creator: the same requests with and without the skill, or old and new version, on Claude Sonnet and Claude Opus, each answer checked against a list of what a good answer contains. Since the CTO and CPTO seats, the grading has been blind.

The honest headline first: plain Claude already has most of these values. On simple requests it passed 22 of 23 checks without the skill. What the skill changes is how the agent acts once the facts are on the table:

What was testedBeforeAfter
Leaders cut their own bonus first (Claude Opus, no skill → skill)0 of 6 runs5 of 6 runs
CTO seat, blind graded (Claude Sonnet, no skill → skill)59 of 84 checks83 of 84 checks
CPTO seat, blind graded (Claude Sonnet, no skill → skill)64 of 81 checks79 of 81 checks
European practice and good leadership (Claude Sonnet, v1.1 → v1.2)128 of 150 checks147 of 150 checks
A flop and a board that wants cuts (Claude Sonnet, v1.2.1 → v1.3)8 of 12 checks12 of 12 checks
Advice to one person (Claude Opus, v1.2.1 → v1.3)29 of 36 checks36 of 36 checks

Version 1.2 cut the runs with a factual error from 6 of 36 to 1. Version 1.3 had leaders own a bad result in 16 of 16 runs where something had gone wrong, against 7 of 16 before.

It wasn't better everywhere. Three of the version 1.3 cases (a board that overrules the alternatives, cuts in Germany, choosing who stays in a Swedish team) barely separated, because the earlier version already got them right. On advice to one person, most of the earlier version's misses were length: it wrote 770 to 1,060 words where about 400 were needed. Each comparison used two or three runs per setup, so read the numbers as direction, not precision. And on Claude the skills cost 9 to 22% more tokens.

It isn't only for Claude. On Gemini, through Google's Antigravity, the skill made an even bigger difference: Gemini 4 Neon passed 80 of 84 checks with it against 51 without, and Gemini 4 Hydrogen 75 against 41, with no factual errors in 28 runs against 6 without. The Gemini runs also caught a weak spot that Claude had read past: a reference file listed the confidential adviser as a place for any disagreement. Version 1.3.1 fixes it. On Gemini the skill roughly doubles the tokens per request (all runs and grades).

So I'd call it a stance and a checklist, not a smarter analyst. It doesn't make the model find more; it makes the agent act more consistently like the leader you'd want. The test cases ship in the repo, so you can rerun them on your own model.

Install and use it

The skills live at github.com/Stukongeluk/the-human-c-suite. In Claude Code, install all three seats as a plugin:

/plugin marketplace add Stukongeluk/the-human-c-suite
/plugin install the-human-c-suite@the-human-c-suite

Or copy a folder from skills/ into ~/.claude/skills/. In the Claude apps, zip a seat's folder and upload it under Customize > Skills (current steps). Then just ask: "The board wants 15% savings, what are our options?" The skills follow the open Agent Skills format, so other agents can load them too; I've tested them on Claude and on Gemini through Google's Antigravity.

What it is not

It's not legal, financial or HR advice: it flags where employment law and works councils come in, and people decide. It's not a way to make layoffs look nicer, and not a surveillance tool. And it doesn't replace leadership: an agent can gather the data and ask the uncomfortable questions, but looking people in the eye is still a human job.

I'm an engineer, not a CEO. I believe the tools we build should make companies better places to work. So try it on your next budget round or reorganisation and tell me where it falls short. Issues and pull requests are welcome; in the spirit of the skills, pushback is the point.
