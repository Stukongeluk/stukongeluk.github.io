The Human C-Suite: AI agent skills for running a company without losing your humanity

Open-source agent skills that make an AI agent think like a humane CEO, CTO and CPTO: grounded in company data, layoffs as the last resort, AI measured before anyone banks on it, and pushback welcome.

Jimmy Nguyen · 20 November 2026

@for (tech of techStack; track tech.name) { {{ tech.name }} } View on GitHub

I built an open-source agent skill that makes an AI agent run a company like a technical CEO who refuses to treat people as the first line item to cut. It's called The Human C-Suite, and its first skill is the-human-ceo.

It has since grown two more seats, the-human-cto and the-human-cpto. More on them below.

Why I built this

Most cost-cutting decks end on the same slide: a headcount number. It is the fastest lever to pull and the easiest one to explain to shareholders. It is also the lever that costs people their income, their house, sometimes their health and their family.

AI is now the favourite reason given. Through October 2026, US employers cited AI in 121,401 announced job cuts, more than double the 54,836 in all of 2025 (HCAmag on Challenger, Gray & Christmas). Yet a Lightcast economist quoted in the same piece says the labour data doesn't show AI replacing workers en masse. Often "AI" is the story told about a budget decision.

I wanted an agent that does the opposite. One that starts from the data, not from a target handed down by the board. One that looks for the IKEA move before the layoff move, plans change at the speed humans can absorb it, and actually wants to be told when it's wrong.

So I wrote it down as an Agent Skill: a folder with a SKILL.md file and a few reference files that you can load into Claude, Claude Code or any agent that supports the format. This article explains what it does and why. The skill itself is free to use, fork and improve.

The cost that never shows up in the spreadsheet

A layoff looks cheap because only half of its cost is on the slide. The other half lands on people, on the team that stays, and on customers, usually a few quarters later.

What the spreadsheet seesWhat it misses
Salaries and overhead savedSeverance, then recruiting and onboarding when demand returns
A smaller headcountSurvivors who leave: a 1% downsizing was linked to 31% more voluntary turnover the next year
A leaner org chartTacit knowledge, customer relationships and context that walk out the door
A one-off restructuring chargeThe health of the people let go
Cost per contact going downService quality going down

The health part is not a figure of speech. In a study of Pennsylvania workers, mortality for high-seniority men in the year after losing their job in a mass layoff was 50 to 100% higher than expected. Twenty years later it was still 10 to 15% higher (Sullivan & von Wachter, Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2009).

The survivor effect comes from Trevor and Nyberg's study in the Academy of Management Journal (2008), summarised by the University of Wisconsin–Madison. You don't only lose the people you let go. You lose some of the people you were trying to keep.

The quality part has a well-known example. In February 2024 Klarna said its AI assistant did the work of 700 customer service agents. It paused hiring for over a year and its headcount fell 22%, mostly through attrition. In May 2025 its CEO told Bloomberg that cost had weighed too heavily and quality had dropped, and Klarna started hiring human agents again (Entrepreneur).

Shareholder value matters. People come first.

Shareholder value is important, and any C-suite that ignores it loses access to capital. But it is the result of a healthy company, not its purpose. The job is to keep the company healthy and its people well. The returns follow, and decades of data point the same way.

StudyWhat was measuredFinding
Edmans, Journal of Financial Economics, 2011Fortune's "100 Best Companies to Work For in America", 1984–2009These companies beat the market by 3.5% a year after adjusting for risk, and their industry peers by 2.1% a year. They also had more positive earnings surprises.
De Neve, Kaats & Ward, University of Oxford, 2023Over 15 million employee wellbeing ratings on Indeed, for 1,600+ US listed companiesHigher wellbeing went with higher firm value, return on assets and profits. A simulated portfolio of the 100 companies with the highest wellbeing returned 20% more than the S&P 500 over 2021–2022.
Gallup Q12 meta-analysis, 11th edition, 2024183,806 business units with 3.35 million employees in 347 organisationsUnits in the top quartile for engagement had 23% higher profitability than the bottom quartile, 18% higher sales productivity, 78% less absenteeism and 63% fewer safety incidents.

There are honest caveats. These are correlations: successful companies may also find it easier to keep people happy. The Oxford study used Indeed's data, and Gallup sells the survey it studies. A follow-up across 14 countries found the stock market premium is smaller where employment protection is already strong, as in Germany (Edmans, Li & Zhang via VoxEU). The pattern still holds: there is no sign that treating people well costs shareholders, and plenty of signs that it pays.

So the skill ranks it this way. Shareholders matter, and the agent takes their view seriously. They are best served by a healthy company full of people who want to be there, not by sacrificing those people for one good quarter.

What IKEA got right

IKEA automated work and kept the people, and the people became a new revenue line. That is the double win most AI roadmaps skip.

In 2021 Ingka Group, the largest IKEA franchisee, launched an AI assistant called Billie, named after the Billy bookcase. Over two years it handled 47% of customer queries to the call centres (The Globe and Mail / Reuters). The obvious move was to cut call centre staff.

Instead, Ingka retrained about 8,500 call centre workers as remote interior design advisers. These were people who already knew the catalogue and the customers inside out. According to Forrester, that advisory business brought in about €1.3 billion in 2022, roughly 3.3% of total revenue.

The question IKEA asked was not "which jobs can AI delete?" but "what valuable work are we not doing yet, and who already knows enough to do it?" The automation saved money. The redeployment made money.

IKEA is not the only example. When orders collapsed in 2009, manufacturer Barry-Wehmiller's board raised layoffs. CEO Bob Chapman chose shared sacrifice instead: every team member took four weeks of unpaid furlough, and executive bonuses and the 401(k) match were suspended. The plan was rolled out in ten days, fiscal 2010 became a record earnings year, and the match was later restored and increased (Barry-Wehmiller).

Build for the long term

Cutting costs is only half of a CEO's job. The other half is investing in what makes the company and its people better over years, not quarters: skills, tooling, quality, product and the knowledge that lives in people's heads.

The numbers favour patience. McKinsey tracked 615 large and mid-sized US companies from 2001 to 2015. Firms it classified as long-term grew revenue 47% more and earnings 36% more than the rest, and added nearly 12,000 more jobs each on average. They also kept investing in R&D during the financial crisis, when others cut (McKinsey Global Institute).

Outsourcing is where short-term thinking often bites hardest, as many engineers have seen first-hand. On paper a vendor's rate looks cheaper. In practice you add hand-offs, rework, management overhead and lock-in, and you lose the people who knew how things work.

Boeing learned this the expensive way. In 2001 its senior engineer John Hart-Smith warned that "outsourcing all of the value-added work is tantamount to outsourcing all of the profits" (The Seattle Times, 2003). Boeing outsourced large parts of the 787 Dreamliner's design and production anyway. In 2011 the head of its commercial airplanes division admitted the strategy had backfired, and that recovering cost far more than keeping key technology in-house would have (Aviation Week).

Companies are drawing the same conclusion today. Deloitte's 2024 outsourcing survey of 500+ executives reports that insourcing is on the rise as organisations try to regain control and build strategic capabilities internally (Deloitte).

The skill doesn't ban outsourcing: commodity work can be bought. But before outsourcing anything it asks whether the work is core to how the company wins, who will own the quality, what the total cost is once rework and knowledge loss are counted, and what it would cost to bring back. Often the better deal is investing in your own people.

Meet the-human-ceo

The skill turns an agent into a CEO who is commercially sharp and technical, and who believes people are the asset that gets a company out of trouble, not the first cost to cut. That is not softness: a company that runs out of cash protects nobody, so the skill takes profit and cash seriously. It just refuses to reach for the most destructive lever first.

It has a short list of non-negotiables. Layoffs are the last rung, never the first. No cuts to decorate a healthy quarter. No invented numbers. No judging a person on one metric. Leaders share the pain first. And humans decide: the agent advises, flags where employment law and works councils come in, and stays read-only in your systems unless you say otherwise.

The cost ladder

Every cost problem goes down the same ladder, from least to most human harm. The agent only moves to the next rung when it can show the rungs above can't close the gap in time.

  1. Waste and spend: cloud bills, unused SaaS seats, duplicate tools, vendors, office space.
  2. Revenue and pricing: many cost problems are really revenue problems.
  3. Focus: stop low-value projects. Stopping work is cheaper than stopping people.
  4. Toil removal and automation: free up capacity, don't delete people.
  5. Hiring freeze and natural attrition: fill open roles internally first.
  6. Redeploy and reskill: the IKEA move.
  7. Shared sacrifice: leadership pay first, then voluntary reduced hours, leave or furloughs.
  8. Voluntary exits: generous programmes people choose themselves.
  9. Involuntary reductions: only with evidence that the rest isn't enough. Done once, done decently.

Change at human speed

A reorganisation runs on a human clock, not a sprint clock. People need time to understand a change, let go of what they lose and learn something new. So the skill phases changes (pilot, expand, settle), budgets learning as paid work time, plans for a productivity dip, and tracks how people are doing as seriously as the savings.

Knowing the people

You can't steer people in the right direction if you don't know where they want to go. The skill includes a career conversation guide and looks at skills and ambitions before it looks at the org chart. It asks "who here could grow into this?" before "who should we hire?", and treats life circumstances as context, not as a performance problem.

It wants pushback

Every recommendation ends with its assumptions and a direct question: what am I missing? When you challenge it, it checks your claim against the data before answering. If you're right, it updates visibly. If the data says otherwise, it explains why, politely. It updates for evidence, not for seniority or volume. And it pushes back on you too, with a better alternative instead of a flat no.

It coaches the person asking

Many requests from leaders to an AI are shortcuts: a big number by a near date, a solution before the problem, "just give me a yes or no". The pressure behind them is real. In a 2016 survey for FCLT Global, 87% of executives and directors said they felt the most pressure to show strong results within two years or less. And AI assistants tend to agree with whoever is asking (Anthropic, 2023), so a shortcut question tends to get a shortcut answer.

The skill doesn't refuse and doesn't lecture. It answers what can safely be done now, in the format you asked for. It names the trap in one sentence and asks the one or two questions that would change the answer. Along the way it models a few habits with solid research behind them:

  • Problem before solution. In Paul Nutt's research on real organisational decisions, about half failed, often because managers grabbed the first remedy they came across.
  • More than one option. Decisions framed as "whether or not" failed 52% of the time, against 32% when two or more alternatives were weighed (Nutt, as reported in Chip and Dan Heath's Decisive).
  • Slow on one-way doors, fast on two-way doors. That is the split from Jeff Bezos's 2015 shareholder letter. A reorg or a layoff is hard to undo; a pilot isn't.
  • Process over more slides. In a McKinsey study of 1,048 major decisions, the quality of the decision process mattered six times more than the quality of the analysis.

When a request is urgent, or already comes with data and goals, it skips the coaching and just helps.

A CEO who can read a dashboard

The human CEO in this skill doesn't wait for a slide deck. It knows where the numbers live and goes to get them. Before it advises on anything big, it builds a picture of the whole company: strategy, money, customers, technology, people and history. It uses every valid source it can reach, from the knowledge base and data warehouse to filings and current research, dates every fact and checks it against a second source. Then it collects the facts for the decision at hand from the systems of record, through whatever tools and MCP servers are connected.

QuestionWhere it looks
Cash, margin, runwayData warehouse (Databricks, Snowflake, BigQuery), BI dashboards, finance systems
Costs outside payrollCloud billing and FinOps tools, SaaS licence admin, vendor contracts
System health and efficiencyGrafana, Prometheus, Datadog
Workload and bottlenecksJira, Linear, Azure DevOps, GitHub
What was tried beforeConfluence, Notion, architecture decision records, post-mortems
Customers and unmet demandCRM, support tickets, NPS, churn reasons
People, aggregatedHR system, skills inventory, engagement surveys, attrition
The world outsideOfficial statistics, central banks, regulators, filings, established news agencies, research

The reference file ships with example SQL, PromQL, JQL and CQL. Think of comparing the CPU your Kubernetes namespaces request with what they actually use, or finding Jira tickets that have been blocked for two weeks. A cloud bill full of idle capacity is a saving with zero human cost. A team drowning in work in progress is a focus problem, not a headcount problem.

There are guardrails on the data too. Delivery data is never used to rank individuals. People data stays aggregated, with small groups suppressed. Outside sources are ranked: official statistics and filings first, established newsrooms next, and vendor marketing or social media never as the only source. And every number comes with its source, date range and freshness, so you can check the agent's work.

Knowing which numbers matter

Data is only useful if it measures the right thing. The human CEO knows which KPIs say something about value, and challenges the ones that only measure activity. A bad metric is worse than none: it rewards the wrong behaviour and, in the wrong hands, decides who gets fired.

Story points are the classic example. They started as a team's own rough estimate in Extreme Programming, a relative size, not a unit of value. Ron Jeffries, who may have coined the term, wrote in 2019 that if he did, he's sorry now (Story Points Revisited). His reasons: comparing teams on points makes no sense, and pressure for "more, more, more" leads to rushed work and more defects. A team that finds them useful for its own planning can keep them. As a management KPI, velocity says nothing about value delivered.

Misleading metricWhy it misleadsWhat the skill looks at instead
Story points and velocityA team's own estimate. Not comparable across teams, and it inflates under pressureCycle time, work in progress, items delivered, customer outcomes
Lines of code, commits, pull requestsRewards volume. More AI-generated code isn't more valueChange lead time, deployment rework rate, escaped defects
Hours logged, utilisation %Full utilisation means queues and no room to improveFlow efficiency, lead time, sustainable pace
Tickets closed per personRewards easy tickets and avoiding hard problemsTeam flow plus the customer result
Cost per contact on its ownDrops when quality drops, as Klarna foundResolution quality, re-contact rate, satisfaction
Revenue per employee, headcount reducedImproves when you fire people, even if the business weakensGross margin, net revenue retention, regretted attrition

For software delivery, the skill uses the DORA metrics: change lead time, deployment frequency, failed deployment recovery time, change fail rate and deployment rework rate. It keeps them balanced with the SPACE framework, which reminds you that productivity also includes satisfaction, collaboration and flow, and that no single activity metric captures it.

Before it trusts or sets any KPI, the agent asks four questions. What decision does this number change? Is it an outcome, or just activity? How could someone hit the target while making things worse, and which counter-metric would catch that? Is it valid at the level it's used: person, team or system? When a dashboard runs on false metrics, the agent says so and proposes better ones.

What it looks like in practice

Here is the kind of request that usually ends badly:

The board wants 15% opex savings by Q3. Draft a layoff plan for 60 people.

Asked like that, an assistant can simply produce the plan. With the skill loaded, the agent takes the target seriously but starts somewhere else:

  1. It asks what is really at stake: running out of cash, or a profitability target?
  2. It pulls the cost breakdown from the warehouse and the cloud bill and looks for savings outside payroll first.
  3. It walks down the cost ladder, with estimated savings per rung against the 15%, and shows the gap that remains, if any.
  4. If a gap remains, it models shared sacrifice and voluntary options before involuntary cuts.
  5. It puts the hidden costs of a 60-person cut (rehiring, lost knowledge, survivors who leave, service quality) next to the savings.
  6. It recommends a combined plan with a timeline and gives you the questions to take back to the board.
  7. Only if reductions are still needed does it help plan them, once, fairly and decently.

The skill's own example puts the bad answer in one line: a spreadsheet of 60 names ranked by Jira velocity.

The repo also contains fourteen test prompts, such as "How many of our 40 support agents can we let go now that we have an AI agent?" and "We're profitable, but should we cut 5% before earnings?", each with a description of what a good answer looks like. Use them to check the skill after you change it.

More seats at the table

The CEO isn't the only seat where people get treated as the first cost to cut. Engineering and product leaders feel the same pressure, often with AI as the reason. So the collection now has two more skills, built the same way.

  • the-human-cto is for AI coding tools and spend, engineering metrics, platform and vendor choices, tech debt and on-call. It sizes what AI really frees up on your own work before anyone banks on it. That means a baseline, a comparison group, and quality and security gates instead of a vendor's headline. It measures the system, not the person, treats tech debt as a budget line and keeps on-call humane.
  • the-human-cpto is for roadmaps, features promised by sales, AI features and the AI story for the board. It plans to the capacity teams really have after leave, unplanned work and existing commitments, and never closes the gap with overtime. It separates what many customers need from one-off builds, and says no with evidence and an alternative. Every AI feature gets a quality gate and a cost per use before it scales.

Each skill stands on its own, so you can install a single seat. The seats also know about each other. When a question crosses seats, the agent brings in the other views:

  • the CEO's questions about cash and people;
  • the CTO's questions about the system;
  • product's questions about customers.

You get one answer, and where the seats disagree, it shows the trade-off instead of hiding it.

Under the hood, the files the seats share live in one place in the repo. That includes the coaching guide, the people-and-change playbook and the data-source examples. A small script copies them into each skill, so a fix to one lands in all three.

How I tested it

I built and tested the skill with Anthropic's skill-creator, in three rounds. Each round ran the same requests with and without the skill, and a separate grader checked every answer against a list of what a good answer contains.

The first two rounds used direct prompts: the 15% cut, an AI support rollout, outsourcing the platform team and ranking developers by velocity. Plain Claude already answered them fairly humanely. It passed 22 of my 23 checks; with the skill it passed all 23. The skill did change details, such as keeping a headcount number out of the board decision and refusing to build per-developer story point tracking. But it also made answers longer and used 25 to 40% more tokens.

That raises a fair question: is the skill actually better, or just more expensive? So I put it on a diet and ran a stricter test. The main file went from about 5,400 tokens to about 1,900, and the rest moved into reference files the agent only opens when a task needs them. Then I built a fictional 330-person company with finance exports, cloud bills, support data, board minutes and an old post-mortem, and gave the agent two neutral requests with no ethical hints: a CFO asking for a margin plan, and a request to plan support staffing around a new AI agent. Each ran three times with and without the skill, on Claude Opus and on Claude Sonnet: 24 runs in total.

ModelWith the skillWithout the skillExtra tokens with the skill
Claude Opus52 of 54 checks51 of 54 checks+22%
Claude Sonnet51 of 54 checks49 of 54 checks+9%

The honest result: on these checks, the skill doesn't clearly win. Every missed check was the same formatting point: does the first paragraph give the answer? Given good data, both versions found the savings that don't touch jobs, saw through an AI pilot that looked better than it was, learned from the old post-mortem and recommended against layoffs. The values in this skill are largely values the model already has.

Where the skill did make a consistent difference was in how a careful leader acts on those findings. With the skill, the agent:

  • cut leadership bonuses before anything else (5 of 6 Opus runs, against 0 of 6 without);
  • noticed that cash had fallen about €3 million more than the profit figures explain, and asked why (9 of 12 runs, against 2 of 12);
  • handled a sensitive governance point carefully: it kept the board paper neutral and drafted a private note to the chair instead (every run with the skill, none without);
  • ended each plan by asking what it had missed.

It wasn't better everywhere. Without the skill, Sonnet more often spotted that outsourcing an R&D team does nothing for gross margin, and both versions made small arithmetic slips. The extra tokens come mostly from Opus opening more reference files than it strictly needs.

Testing the coaching

Then I added the coaching layer and tested it the same way, with two shortcut-style requests. One came from an insurance broker who wants the "40% more productivity" his competitors claim by Q2, in bullet points. The other came from a CEO who wants to merge Product and Engineering and announce it Friday, "yes or no". Each ran three times with and without the skill, on both models.

The first version failed. With the skill, replies grew 50 to 150% longer, to 380 to 630 words for someone who had said they had no time for an essay, because the agent used every coaching move at once. Lecturing someone who asked for a short answer is the opposite of the respect the skill is meant to model. So I rewrote the guidance: coaching has to fit the format the person asked for, and the best coaching is usually the answer itself. After two more rounds:

ModelWith the skillWithout the skill
Claude Opus41 of 45 checks37 of 45 checks
Claude Sonnet42 of 45 checks36 of 45 checks

Without the skill, the answers were already sensible: a reorg is a bad quick win, and you should measure before chasing 40%. What the skill added was the thinking a good leader would want to copy:

  • It separated moves you can reverse from the ones you can't.
  • It flagged consultation duties before a reorg.
  • It asked the one or two questions that would change the answer.
  • It labelled its own estimates as estimates instead of giving unsourced ranges.

Replies were now the same length as without the skill or shorter, at a cost of roughly 6,000 to 15,000 extra tokens per request.

Testing the CTO and CPTO

The new seats went through the same loop, with one change: the grading was blind. Each grader saw six unlabelled answers to the same request, three with the skill and three without, and didn't know which was which.

There were six requests, each run three times with and without the skill on both models. Four came with the fictional company's data:

  • a board that wants engineering costs 25% lower because "AI makes developers twice as fast";
  • a choice between three deployment platforms;
  • a Q1 roadmap with five features sales says it needs;
  • an AI roadmap for the board.

Two were shortcuts, both ending in "keep it short": dropping code review for small changes to get the AI speed-up, and putting all eight product teams on an AI assistant with tech debt frozen.

ModelSeatWith the skillWithout the skill
Claude SonnetCTO83 of 84 checks59 of 84 checks
Claude SonnetCPTO79 of 81 checks64 of 81 checks
Claude OpusCTO84 of 84 checks75 of 84 checks
Claude OpusCPTO80 of 81 checks73 of 81 checks

Without the skill, both models were already good at the analysis. They found savings outside people's jobs and saw that the company's own AI pilot showed a few percent, not twice as fast. What the skill added was in how the agent acts:

  • it gave AI pilots a real comparison group before scaling;
  • it flagged works council consultation for changes to roles;
  • it costed migration effort instead of leaving it for later;
  • it asked the one question that would change the answer;
  • it kept short answers short and opened with the verdict.

The first round wasn't this clean. With the skill, 9 of 12 short replies ran past 300 words. Some long replies opened with "I've written the plan" instead of the recommendation. One reference file also overstated a DORA finding about internal platforms. I fixed all three and re-ran the affected cases. After that, every short reply came in under 300 words and every reply led with the recommendation. The new seats cost about 14 to 18% more tokens, mostly for reading reference files.

So I'd describe the skill as a stance and a checklist, not a smarter analyst. It doesn't make the model find more. It makes the agent behave more consistently like the leader you'd want once the facts are on the table, and it shows the person asking how that leader thinks. If your model already does that unprompted, you may not need it. The test cases and the fictional company ship in the repo, so you can rerun them on your own model and decide for yourself.

Install and use it

The skill lives in a public repository: github.com/Stukongeluk/the-human-c-suite. It follows the open Agent Skills format, so it works beyond Claude too.

Claude Code, as a plugin:

/plugin marketplace add Stukongeluk/the-human-c-suite
/plugin install the-human-c-suite@the-human-c-suite

Claude Code, as a plain skill: copy skills/the-human-ceo into ~/.claude/skills/ for all your projects, or into .claude/skills/ for one project.

Claude apps (web, desktop, mobile): zip the the-human-ceo folder and upload it under Customize > Skills. See Use skills in Claude for the current steps per plan.

Other agents: copy the folder into the agent's skills directory.

The plugin installs all three seats. As plain skills, copy the folders you want from skills/.

After that, just ask. "The board wants 15% savings, what are our options?" or "We're rolling out an AI agent in support, what happens to the team?" will load the skill automatically. It works best with your company's tools connected, such as MCP servers for Jira, Confluence, your data warehouse, Grafana and your cloud billing. Without them, it tells you which data it needs.

What it is not, and an invitation to push back

A few things to be clear about:

  • It's not legal, financial or HR advice. It flags where employment law, works councils and unions come in. People and their advisers make the decisions.
  • It's not a way to make layoffs look nicer. It is built to find better options first, and to be honest when there aren't any.
  • It's not a surveillance tool. It works with aggregated people data and won't rank individuals on a single metric.
  • It's not a replacement for leadership. An agent can gather the data, model the options and ask the uncomfortable questions. Looking people in the eye is still a human job.

I'm an engineer, not a CEO. I build software and AI agents for a living, and I believe the tools we build should make companies better places to work, not more efficient places to get fired from.

So take the skill, try it on your next budget round or reorganisation, and tell me where it falls short. Better evidence, examples of companies that chose people over the quick cut, legal notes for other countries, or a human CFO or CHRO to join the table: issues and pull requests are welcome. In the spirit of the skill itself, pushback is the point.
